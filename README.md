# DS1 FPS Unlock: PTDE and Remastered

High-FPS unlock with gameplay physics fixes for **Dark Souls: Remastered** and **Dark Souls: Prepare to Die Edition**. Movement, physics, camera, animation and menus stay correct at any frame rate, not just faster. Both games run in real time at 120, 240 or whatever your PC holds, and keep real-time speed when the frame rate dips.

The mods are proxy DLLs. The game's data files are never modified; the one change on disk is PTDE's 4 GB flag in DARKSOULS.exe (`LargeAddressAware`, the original kept as `DARKSOULS.exe.before-laa`). Each mod has an INI with a switch for every fix so you can compare against the retail behaviour.

Looking for co-op in Prepare to Die Edition? [Prepare to Co-op Edition](https://github.com/zofrea1/DS1-Prepare-to-Coop-PTDE) plays the whole game with up to seven friends and includes this mod.

Looking for quality-of-life features in Prepare to Die Edition? [DSfix 3.0](https://www.nexusmods.com/darksouls/mods/1985) by DDTM adds them (item sorting, a bonfire map, keyboard and mouse controls and more) and works together with this mod: see [DSfix 3.0](#dsfix-30).

The original game's 30 FPS physics were tuned for 30 frames per second; both mods are exact at the native rate and very close at every other rate. See [Fix details](#fix-details) for what each fix does.

**Accuracy.** This is very close to the game at its native rate, and more than good enough for casual and serious play. It is not a perfect 1:1 match: retail decides some of these things on a fixed frame grid, so a borderline slope or lip can behave slightly differently at 180 or 240 FPS. Speedrunners and anyone who needs stock-exact precision should not use this.

## Fix details

**Screens that are not 16:9 (`FixAspect`, `FixAspectUI`, `UIScale`, Prepare to Die Edition).** The projection is locked to 16:9, so on a 16:10 display (the Steam Deck's 1280x800, or 1920x1200) the extra height is black bars, and on an ultrawide display (3440x1440, 5120x1440) the extra width is. The in-game list often stops at 1280x720, which is why DSfix's borderless mode cannot be pointed at 1280x800 from the menu. When the display is not 16:9 and the game asked for a 16:9 mode, the swap chain is sized to the display and the game's screen cameras are given the display's aspect: on a taller screen the vertical field of view is opened so the horizontal view stays the same (on 16:10, 43 to 47.3 degrees); on a wider screen the vertical view stays and the horizontal one opens (at 3440x1440, 70 to 86.5 degrees). The world and the effects use that one projection. The game lays its HUD and menus out on 1280x720 and would stretch that to the screen's shape (11% taller on 16:10, a third wider at 21:9); with `FixAspectUI` (the default) the layout is drawn at its 16:9 shape in the middle of the picture, full-screen fades and backgrounds still cover the whole screen, and the marks that follow something in the world (lock-on, health bars over characters, damage numbers) are placed over their targets. A health bar over a target in the side margins of an ultrawide picture is not drawn (the game hides bars outside its layout). Movies are stretched to fill the screen. `UIScale` and `UIScaleWorld` are described below. A 16:9 display is left alone by `FixAspect`. Leave DSfix `presentWidth` and `presentHeight` at 0. With `YieldToDSFix = true` beside DSfix, `FixAspect` and `FixAspectUI` are off, so that DSfix 3.0's HUD mod finds the HUD at the game's own places (see [Prepare to Die Edition and DSfix](#prepare-to-die-edition-and-dsfix)). On a Steam Deck whose game resolution is set to 1280x720, the mod asks for 1280x800; if that picture looks squashed, set the game resolution in Steam to 1280x800.

**HUD and menu size (`UIScale`, `UIScaleWorld`, `UIScaleText`, `UIScaleMenus`, `FixBossBarOverlap`, Prepare to Die Edition).** The game's menus and HUD are dialogs of FROM's menu system (EzMenu), each an object of its own class, drawn through one device context whose current transform places every image and text drawn in it. The HUD is one whole-screen dialog (`FrpgMenuDlgPCGauge`, whose own pieces are the health and stamina bars) with the rest of the HUD as dialogs inside it: the humanity counter and status icons (`FrpgMenuDlgFEPlayerState`), each equipment slot (`FrpgMenuDlgFeShortcut`), the soul counter (`FrpgMenuDlgFEGetSoul`), the boss bars, the build-up meters, the condition notices, the lock-on marker and the enemies' bars. When a scaled dialog starts to draw, the transform it inherits gets a scale about its anchor (a corner or edge of the screen for the HUD, its own middle for a mark over something in the world), so all of it, its images, texts and the dialogs inside it, is placed smaller or larger by the game's own arithmetic; a dialog inside a scaled one takes its own anchor instead, and the HUD dialog's own pieces are anchored by their names in the menu file (the item and spell names go with the equipment slots). Until 2.4.1 the mod found the HUD's pieces by where they were drawn on the screen and moved their vertices, which mixed up pieces that met (an enemy's bar passing the status icons or a build-up meter, the HUD behind a menu); now the game says which dialog every piece belongs to. `UIScale` (percent, 50 to 150): the health and stamina bars with the status icons toward the top left corner, the equipment slots with the item and spell names toward the bottom left, the soul counter and the souls just gained toward the bottom right, a boss's health bar and the prompts toward the bottom edge, the build-up meters and notices such as "POISONED" about their own place. The HUD keeps that size while a menu is open; banners (YOU DIED, area names) keep the game's size. `UIScaleWorld` (percent, 50 to 150): enemies' and other players' floating health bars with their names and damage numbers, and the lock-on marker, each about its own middle. Seen in game (2026-11-17, Firelink and Blighttown at 4K): the HUD at 50, 60 and 130 percent; the start menu and the inventory over the scaled HUD; a poison build-up meter, and a boss bar with a prompt over it; the lock-on marker and an enemy's bar with its damage number at 60. `UIScaleText`: the letters. The transform places a text's box, not the size of its letters, which the game takes from one of its 13 font sizes when a run of text begins (`FrpgMenuFontPrinter`); so in the first 2.4.2 test builds the letters kept the game's size in a smaller HUD (two prompt lines ran into each other, the soul count overfilled its box, a damage number dwarfed its bar). After each run of text begun in a scaled dialog, a command of the mod's own in the menus' command buffer multiplies the text renderer's letter scale and line height by the text's size, so the letters and the button icons inside a text shrink or grow with their box. `auto` (the default) gives each text its dialog's size; a number (50 to 150) is the letters' size in percent of the game's for every text in the HUD and the marks, whatever their parts' size. The menus' letters keep the game's size. Seen in game (2026-11-17, 2048x1152): the item windows, the prompt with two lines, the equipment slots' names and counts and the soul count at 60, 100 and 130 percent and with letters at 80 in a HUD at 60; a boss's name under a prompt; a damage number over an enemy's bar at 60; the inventory over a HUD at 60 with its letters as the game's. `UIScaleMenus` (percent, 50 to 150; `auto`, the default, follows `UIScale`; `false` keeps the game's size): the in-game menus, scaled about the middle of the screen so they show more of the game on all four sides alike (Remastered's menu scale shrinks toward a corner). Their screens are plain dialogs found by their names in the menu file and by the order the game draws them in: in the world (the HUD `F20` drawn), every top-level dialog drawn after the back plate `R11` is a menu's, apart from the field's layers (`F...`: prompts, item windows, banners and the recall stay the HUD's) and the movie layer. That is the start menu `R00` with the screen it opened (the items `I01`, the equipment `I02`, the status `R02`, the system `I06`, the options `R01`, the brightness `T07`, their submenus), the bonfire's and the shops' screens (Level Up `C01`, Attune Magic `C05`, the Bottomless Box `C07`, Repair `S01`), the icon layers drawn with them; plus the talk group `F04` (the list of choices at a bonfire or with a character `F04-01`, and what a character says `F04-00`), the message box with its OK (`F22`, "X has died") and the button guide and help line `R04`, which the game draws first in each frame (so it is scaled while a menu was up in the last one). The game's general dialogs (`R03`, `FrpgMenuDlgGeneralOwner`, drawn before the HUD in each frame and shown all the time) kept the game's size up to 2.7.0: its questions with OK and CANCEL (`R03-00` to `R03-12`; "Are you sure you want to quit the game?" is `R03-02`) are now menus about the middle of the screen while the last frame was in the world (not at the title), the mouse and the button guide with them, and its plates in the prompts' place (`R03-20` to `R03-22`, one or two lines: "Used Dungeon Cell Key") go as the prompts do (`UIScale` toward the bottom edge, moved up with them above a boss's bar). Each kind of menu shrinks toward its own anchor: the screens toward the middle of the screen; the start menu on its own toward its box's top right corner (with a screen open, it goes with the screen); the bonfire's menu and the list of choices toward their box's top left corner; the message box toward the bottom edge. What a character says keeps its place and width (its backdrop darkens the whole bottom of the screen) and only its letters take the menus' size. The button guide and the icon layers go with the kind of menu shown. Inside a scaled menu, a dialog the game parks wholly off its screen (Repair's second cursor, at -63,-123) is not drawn, as the menu scaled toward the middle would bring it into view. The menus' letters go with them. The mouse: the game turns the pointer's place into the layout's units once a frame (and the menus test it against their dialogs' rectangles); while a scaled menu is up that place is mapped back into the menu's own units (about that menu's anchor), so hovering and clicking land on what is drawn. `FixBossBarOverlap` (default on): the game places the prompt box (563 to 638 in its 1280x720 layout, to 663 with two lines) over a boss's health bar (the name starts at 560), so while a bar is shown the prompt and the item windows above it are moved up until the box ends above the highest boss name; it works at any `UIScale` (both scale toward the bottom edge). Seen in game (2026-11-17): the inventory, an item's command menu, the equipment and its item list, the status, the system, the options and the brightness screens at 75 with the HUD at 100; the mouse over the fourth item of the scaled inventory list selected it; the bonfire's list, Level Up, Repair, Attune Magic and the Bottomless Box at 75 (the mouse over the drawn fourth row of the bonfire's list, scaled toward its top left corner, selected it), the start menu toward its corner, a message box, and the Crestfallen Warrior's line at 75; one and two boss bars with a one- and a two-line prompt, at 100 and 60 percent; (2026-11-21) the quit question over the system screen and "Used Dungeon Cell Key" at the Undead Asylum's cell door, at 60. Where the menus' code is not this build's, the earlier screen-position rules are used (50 to 100 only).

**Black specks with anti-aliasing (`FixAntialiasingDepth`, Prepare to Die Edition).** With anti-aliasing on, the game draws the scene's depth a second time into a multisampled 8-bit target, as the depth z/w in three base-256 digits across red, green and blue, and resolves it (each pixel's samples averaged) into the texture that the low-resolution effects, their upsample and the depth of field read. Where a pixel's samples lie on both sides of a step of the top digit (the edge of a grass blade in front of the ground), the averaged digits are rounded to 8 bits and decode up to 1/512 of z/w beyond both samples. z/w crosses those steps 12.8, 6.4, 4.3 ... m from the camera, so in a ring around the player the depth at blade edges was off by up to several metres, and the mist and smoke laid back over the scene by that depth left black specks there (at Firelink Shrine: a grid of them with the game's 512x360 effects, finer ones at `EffectsResolution` half). The game as shipped does it too; a higher resolution and more samples only make it easier to see. The depth is kept as one 32-bit float per sample instead (the same size as the game's 8-bit target): the game's shaders that draw it get copies that write the depth their three digits hold as that number, the resolve then averages the samples' depths exactly, and every reader decodes it unchanged (a float target reads back as (x, 1, 1)); the one pass that copies the packed depth as it is, into the effects' low-resolution depth, gets a copy that writes the game's three digits again. Measured at Firelink at 4K with 4x anti-aliasing (2026-11-16): pixels whose depth lay beyond all eight neighbours, 14,214 before and 158 after (thin blade tips), at the same frame rate (213 and 216 FPS on an RTX 3090, within the noise). The first version (2.4.2 test 13) kept the three digits in 16 bits per channel: exact too, but twice the memory at four samples a pixel, 203 against 179 FPS in the same place. It stays as the fallback where the graphics driver cannot multisample or filter a float target, and the log says which is used. Under DXVK (the Steam Deck and Linux, or `DXVK = true`), up to 2.9.0, grass and leaves could also leave grey specks while the camera moved: pixels where the scene's background showed through. DXVK makes the alpha test at the precision of the target being drawn (the game's test passes above 128 of 255: a float target passes from 0.50196, a 10-bit one from 0.50293), so the pre-pass that draws this depth let edge pixels through that the colour pass then left out, and the colour pass draws only where the depth matches. Under DXVK the pre-pass now makes the colour pass's test itself (the alpha rounded at the colour target's precision, watched every frame); measured over 12 moving frames each, 0 specks against 1 to 7, at no measurable cost.

**Colour depth (`HighPrecisionColor`, Prepare to Die Edition).** The game keeps its scene in 8 bits per colour, in the screen's gamma: every layer of fog, smoke or glow blended into it is rounded to 1/255, and the final composite then scales the scene (by 1.3 at Firelink) and grades it, which widens those steps into bands in thin fog and dark gradients and into rings around a pulsing glow. `true` (the default) makes the scene's colour targets 10-bit (`A2R10G10B10`, in the game's own format table): the same 32 bits a pixel as the game's targets, so the same memory and bandwidth, with four times the steps, and the final composite dithers the result into the 8-bit frame. Their alpha has 2 bits, and the game keeps one value there: after the scene its depth of field draws its far blur rate into the scene's alpha alone (from the depth) and reads it back. That draw goes into an 8-bit texture of its own (the game's precision), and the shaders that read the rate take it from there; nothing else reads the scene's alpha (the scene's own draws write colour only, and the glare makes its own). `float` uses 16-bit float targets (twice the memory and bandwidth). Measured at Firelink (2026-11-16, RTX 3090, DXVK, 4x anti-aliasing, 5760x3240 so the GPU is the limit): 8-bit 119.5 FPS, 10-bit 115.0, float 102.8, i.e. 0.33 ms and 1.36 ms a frame; 4K has 0.44 times the pixels, so about 0.15 ms and 0.6 ms (3% and 12% at 200 FPS). The fogged wall in front of the spawn point, contrast-stretched: 71% of neighbouring pixels equal in 8 bits (flat steps), 21% in 10 bits, 22% in float, and the 10-bit and float pictures match within the frame-to-frame noise, the depth of field's distance blur included. Where the graphics driver cannot blend into, filter or multisample a 10-bit target at `AntialiasingSamples`, `true` uses float, and the log says so.

**The dither, and 10 bits to the screen (`HighPrecisionOutput`, Prepare to Die Edition).** Up to 2.9.0 the composite's dither into the 8-bit frame was an ordered 4x4 one of half an 8-bit step: where a dark value fell between two steps, every second pixel sat one step above its neighbours, the same step in red, green and blue, so it read as a lighter, whitish dot, and the pattern was fixed to the screen, so it crawled as the camera moved. At 4K in the Undead Asylum's opening cutscene 4.4% of the dark pixels were such dots. Shown on a display (one step at 8-bit level 8 to 9, in the PQ scale's just-noticeable steps) they measured +24 to +33 steps in SDR at 150 to 400 nits and +15 to +24 in Windows HDR at 80 to 480 nits: the near-black of SDR's gamma 2.2 makes one step a larger jump. The dither is now an interleaved gradient noise per channel, moved on every frame: in one frame the same frozen view has a third fewer specks and none where all three channels step together, and averaged over 4 frames (17 ms at 240 Hz, 67 ms at 60), as the eye sees them, 1,752 specks of half a step or more per million dark pixels are left against the ordered dither's 62,178, 206 over 8 frames. With `HighPrecisionOutput` the game's frame buffer (the final composite's and the HUD's target, made in CoreGraphics' init, where one format index also gives the game's back buffer format) is `A2R10G10B10`, the device's back buffer too, and DXVK presents a 10-bit swap chain (through DXGI as `R10G10B10A2` with `DxvkPresentThroughDxgi`); the dither is then a 10-bit step. Same frame: no dots left (0 per million dark pixels, from 44,126 with the ordered dither), the same memory and bandwidth. `auto` (the default) does this where Windows composes the display above 8 bits (HDR or Auto Color Management on), with DXVK and not beside DSfix; `true` with DXVK whatever the display; `false` keeps the 8-bit picture. Under Proton (the Steam Deck, Linux) the game's Direct3D 9 is Proton's own DXVK, which falls back to an 8-bit swap chain, silently, when the window's Vulkan surface offers no 10-bit format, so before the device is made a temporary Vulkan surface on the game window (the same loader and layers, gamescope's included) is asked for its formats, and the picture goes out in 10 bits only when a 10-bit one in the SDR colour space is offered (with HDR on in `auto`; the log names the formats).

**Shadows (`ShadowMapResolution`, Prepare to Die Edition).** Characters and objects compare their depth from the light with one 2048x2048 shadow map. On a lit, rounded surface that faces the light at a shallow angle the comparison lands on the map's own texels and shows them as a fine weave (seen on the egg sacs in Quelaag's Domain). `ShadowMapResolution` makes the map larger (the game's size is one number, read when the map is made): 4096 by default, 8192 possible, 2048 the game's own (with `YieldToDSFix = true` beside DSfix, always the game's own). The weave is finer and shadow edges are sharper, at more GPU time and video memory (about 130 MB at 4096 and 510 MB at 8192). `ShadowBias` (2.3.0 to 2.5.0) is gone: it moved the comparison toward the light by a fixed amount in the light's depth, which near the camera came to some 20 cm of height and erased real shadows (an egg carrier's top no longer shadowed its lower sacs), and only one of the game's two shadow lookups had it, so characters lit up or darkened as they crossed the edges of the map's slices. An INI that still lists it is told so once in the log.

**Start-up notice and logos (`SkipIntro`).** At every start Prepare to Die Edition's title screen shows the autosave notice ("This game uses an autosave function...") for 10 seconds or until a button is pressed, checks the save data, then opens the logo screen and waits for its last logo. With `SkipIntro` neither the notice nor the logo screen is opened, so the save data check and then the title screen come up straight away. DSfix's `skipIntro` instead speeds up the game's clock while it sees the Bandai Namco logo, which depends on the game's timing and does not work on every setup. Remastered on PC shows no notice: its start-up opens the logo screen and waits for it to close. With `SkipIntro` (since 2.0.0) that screen is never opened and the title screen follows at once. The step that opens it is replaced in the game's table of start-up steps rather than patched, so it works alongside Seamless Co-op. Both work with or without `FPSUnlock`.

**Offline start (`StartOffline`, Remastered).** After the title screen, Remastered reads its launch setting (Network settings, Launch setting) to decide whether to log in to the online service. With `StartOffline` (on by default since 2.0.0) the mod sets it to Start Offline just before the game reads it, so the game takes its own offline path and the Network settings show Start Offline. Playing offline keeps the modded game off the official servers, where players have reported temporary bans. It does nothing when Seamless Co-op is loaded, which has its own networking. It works with or without `FPSUnlock`. To play online, set it to `false` and set Launch setting back to Start Online in the game (the game may have saved Start Offline). `DisableOnlineMode` is accepted as another name.

**Controller (`FixController`, Prepare to Die Edition).** The game decides a DirectInput device is an XInput pad only when its id is one of four values from Windows 7, one per player slot. A current pad does not have those ids, so the game reads it through DirectInput, which is unreliable, and whichever device was enumerated first wins. A second controller, a wheel or a virtual pad is enough to make the real one go dead. The mod still honours those four ids when the device behind one is actually connected. Otherwise it binds a gamepad, preferring the one being held, and a slot that drops out for a moment is kept for half a second before another connected gamepad is tried. Wheels, flight sticks and music controllers are not taken. On the Steam Deck the same DLL has to forward XInput to Wine's controller backend (`xinput1_4`); forwarding to `xinput1_3` calls this mod again and the deck's pad never appears. That forward happens whether or not `FixController` is on. The Deck also needs the launch option `WINEDLLOVERRIDES="xinput1_3=n,b" %command%` so Wine loads this DLL.

**Steam's pause (`SteamPauseGuard`, Prepare to Die Edition).** On Windows the Steam client checks Wi-Fi about every 5 seconds (`WlanOpenHandle`, a call to the WLAN AutoConfig service) on the thread that answers games, so for about 15 ms every Steam call any game makes waits. The game makes two every frame: `SteamAPI_RunCallbacks`, and its matchmaking task's lobby distance filter, set again every frame though only a lobby search uses it. At its original 30 FPS the wait fit inside the frame; above 60 FPS it is a visible hitch every 5 seconds, with Steam online or offline, with or without Steam Input. In an online session the game also sends its packets (positions and the like) from the game thread every frame. The guard keeps the filter and hands it to Steam just before the next lobby search; sends the game's packets from a thread of its own, in order (the game is told they went at once); and lets the callbacks through only right after Steam has answered quickly: a small thread asks Steam the time every 10 ms, and the callbacks go in the 1.5 ms after a quick answer, about 30 times a second, but only on a PC where Steam has paused in the last minute. A pause would have to begin inside that sliver to catch a frame. On the Steam Deck it is off (Proton's Steam has no such pause). Measured on a Ryzen 7 5800X3D with an Intel AX200: Steam's pause 12-20 ms every 5.00-5.05 s.

**Lock-on camera (`FixCamera`).** The follow camera smooths several things toward a target by a fixed fraction every frame, tuned for the native frame rate: the yaw and pitch, the point it looks at (0.4 sideways, 0.3 vertically while locked on), the camera distance, the pivot that follows your character, the settling back after you let go of the stick, the automatic turn toward your walking direction, stick smoothing and the blend between camera settings when locking on. Every one of them now takes the same real time at any frame rate. Earlier versions scaled only the first two, so a pan from target to target still finished noticeably early.

Turning the camera with the stick or mouse also raises how tightly it follows, for two seconds, then fades that boost out over one second. The timer is real time and keeps running after you lock on. Before 1.5.0 only the base weight was scaled, so during those seconds the camera followed several times too fast above the native rate. The boost is scaled the same way now.

**Lock-on body turn (`FixLockOnTurn`).** A small bone controller bends the hips, spine and head toward an angle: your torso and head toward the locked-on target (including while strafing), your upper body when aiming a bow or crossbow, and an enemy's head and upper body while it tracks you. It moved each bone a fixed fraction of the way every frame (0.6 hips, 0.2 head, 0.1 spine), so at 120 FPS the bending happened four times faster than in the game. Weapons ride on the skeleton, so this is not only cosmetic: enemies' upper bodies tracked you more sharply than in the game. The fix restores the native timing. The whole-body turn (turn speed in degrees per second) was already correct and is unchanged.

**Remastered lock-on speed (`LockOnPtdeSpeed`).** Remastered kept the original game's per-frame camera and body-turn values but runs them at 60 FPS, so its lock-on pan and body turn are twice as fast as Prepare to Die Edition. The INI included with 1.5.0 sets `LockOnPtdeSpeed = true`, which uses the original game's speed for both (that includes enemies' head and upper-body tracking). Set it to `false` for Remastered's own speed. The old name `CameraPtdeSpeed` still works. Since 1.8.0 a missing key also means `true`, as for the movement switches below (1.4.0 to 1.7.0 kept Remastered's speed when the key was missing).

**Original game movement (`SlideFrictionPtde`, `GrazePtde`, `StepDownPtde`, `StepHeightPtde`, `AirDampingPtde`, Remastered).** Remastered's movement code is the original game's, branch for branch, and its movement data is the same. When it moved to 60 FPS, some per-frame values were halved so that they kept their effect per second (slope gravity, the graze speed test). Others were left as they were, so Remastered applies them twice as often as the original game. Since 1.8.0 each of these has a switch that uses the original game's value:

- `SlideFrictionPtde`: slide speed left after sliding off a steep slope is multiplied by 0.65 every frame, so Remastered stops it twice as fast. Remastered also zeroes it below 0.07 m/s, where the original game stops it at 0.1 m/s. Needs `FixSlide`.
- `GrazePtde`: running into a wall or a corner lowers the speed multiplier by 0.8 a frame, down to half speed, and it recovers by 1.2 a frame afterwards. Remastered reaches half speed in about 50 ms and recovers in about 65 ms; the original game takes about 100 ms and 130 ms. Needs `FixGraze`.
- `StepDownPtde`: the ground snap pulls the character down onto falling ground by at most 0.4 m a frame. The original game lets go of ground that drops away faster than 12 m/s, Remastered only above 24 m/s, so on a rounded ledge or a steep slope the original game's character starts to fall sooner. Needs `FixStepDown`.
- `StepHeightPtde`: the highest step the character climbs without jumping is 0.30 m in the original game and 0.31 m in Remastered.
- `AirDampingPtde`: momentum in the air after walking or running off a ledge fades by 0.95 per 1/30 s in the original game and 0.975 per 1/60 s in Remastered, about 2% less over a second. Needs `FixDamping`.

`HudPtdeSpeed` does the same for the HUD bars: Remastered kept the original game's per-frame steps for the health, stamina and boss bars, so an Estus heal fills the bar and the yellow part of the stamina bar drains away twice as fast as in the original game. With it on (and `FixUI`) the bars move at the original game's speed.

All six are on in the included INI, and also when a key is missing (since 1.8.0 `LockOnPtdeSpeed` and `FootIkPtdeSpeed` are too). Set one to `false` for Remastered's own behaviour. The two games also use different versions of Havok (2010 and 2015) for collisions against walls and corners, which these switches do not change.

**Foot IK (`FixFootIk`).** On stairs and slopes Havok's foot placement puts each foot on the ground and the game lowers the hips so both feet reach. Three steps of it moved a fixed fraction per frame: the hips toward the height the feet need (0.2 of the gap a frame), the hips back to normal when neither foot is on the ground (0.6 left a frame), and the blend of the foot placement in and out (0.2 a frame). At a high frame rate the hips followed each step of a staircase several times faster than at the native rate, so running up or down stairs and slopes bobbed. Each fraction now covers the same real time at any frame rate. Remastered kept the original game's per-frame values at 60 FPS, so its hips settle twice as fast as in the original game; `FootIkPtdeSpeed` (on by default) uses the original game's speed. Remastered's Havok also turns each foot toward the slope a fraction per frame; that follows the frame time at Remastered's own speed, since the original game's Havok has no such step.

**NPCs' feet (`FootIkNpcs`).** The foot placement above belongs to every human character (your own, the other players' and the human NPCs': enemies have none), but the game runs it for your character only, so everyone else stood on stairs, slopes and roofs with the feet of their animation for flat ground. Prepare to Die Edition now runs it for the human NPCs too, the summoned phantoms among them, and Prepare to Co-op for the other players' characters (`FootIkPlayers`): the same placement, with the same frame-time fix. It costs about 14 microseconds of the game thread a frame for each human the game moves every frame (those near you; on a Ryzen 7 5800X3D seven NPCs side by side added 0.1 ms, against 9 microseconds each for the game's own work on them without it, and the frame rate did not change). Sitting NPCs keep their pose.

**Running jumps (`FixJump`, Remastered).** A running jump does not leave the ground. For the first frames it follows the jump animation, then it keeps the speed from the end of that take-off. At about 30 FPS the take-off is only a few frames, and one of them can have no forward motion. The jump then alternated between stopped and full speed and covered about half the distance, and the sprint-slowdown check cut it further. `FixJump` keeps the faster of those two speeds for the rest of the jump. The height of the arc is unchanged. At 60 FPS and above the two speeds already match, so those jumps are left as they are. Prepare to Die Edition's native rate is 30, and it does not have this key.

**Ghost replays (`FixGhosts`).** Bloodstain replays and wandering ghosts step to the next recorded sample every 10 frames in Prepare to Die Edition and every 20 in Remastered, one sample per third of a second at the native rate. At 120 FPS they played several times too fast. The recorder that captures your own bloodstain and the ghost data sent to other players counts in frames too, so a high frame rate recorded it too densely and other players saw your ghosts in slow motion. Playback and recording now follow real time, matching the native rate exactly.

Between samples a replay turns toward the next recorded facing by a stored amount every frame: a tenth of the turn in Prepare to Die Edition (ten frames per sample) and a twentieth in Remastered. Other players' characters do the same with each network update: a fifth (Prepare to Die Edition) or a tenth (Remastered) of the turn per frame, an update arriving every sixth of a second at the native rate. At a higher frame rate those amounts added up to several times the turn before the next update came, so ghosts and other players spun past their facing and snapped back. The amount is now scaled by the frame time, so each turn takes the time between updates, as at the native rate.

The same sample also stores a step toward the next recorded position. The game reads that step as a direction and a walk or run speed, and it also moves the body by a copy of it every frame. Above the native rate a ghost crossed the gap in the first few frames and then stood still until the next sample, while the walk or run animation kept playing. The copy is now scaled by the frame time, so the ghost moves evenly across the third of a second between samples. The direction and the walk or run speed still come from the unscaled step.

In Prepare to Die Edition another player's character walks, runs or sprints by how far it moved toward its last received position this frame, times 30: a speed in metres per second at the native rate. At a higher frame rate a frame's step is smaller, so a running player was shown walking and a sprint showed only for the odd frame. The factor is now the real frames per second.

**Particle effects (`FixSfxSpawnRate`, Prepare to Die Edition).** Effect files give each emitter a spawn interval. Many use 1/60 s, or 0 for "every frame", which at the native 30 FPS meant one spawn per frame. At a higher frame rate those emitters spawn once per frame, so at 120 FPS smoke, sparks and embers came out up to four times as thick and bright. Remastered fixed this in its data when it moved to 60 FPS: every interval below 1/30 s became exactly 1/30 s (about 3,500 values) and nothing else changed. The mod applies the same rule to each effect as the game loads it, so emitters spawn as densely as at the native rate. It sets those intervals a hair under 1/30 s (0.95/30 s): an emitter spawns once the time since its last spawn reaches its interval, and exactly 1/30 s sits on the 30 FPS frame, so a frame that came a fraction of a millisecond early spawned nothing. Up to 2.4.1 that thinned these effects at every frame rate: particles emitted a second at the Demon Ruins bonfire (2026-11-16) were 878 in the game as shipped at 30 FPS, and with the 1/30 s rule 646 at 30 FPS and 654 at 60. Now they are 877 at 30, 884 at 60, 862 at 144 and 864 at 240 (as shipped: 1,123 at 60, 1,301 at 144 and 1,374 at 240), and in Quelaag's Domain 715 at 30 against 716 at 60 and 728 at 240 (as shipped: 1,148 at 240). Remastered's effect data already has the 1/30 s rule (3,247 intervals, and the Prepare to Die Edition effects mod applies it to the effects it ports), and its other intervals are whole 60 FPS frames (3,179), so at its own 60 FPS each of them sits on a frame's end the same way. Its effect state machine (the same code as the original game's) compares the timer with the interval and restarts it at 0, so there `FixSfxSpawnRate` lets the timer fire up to 1/600 s early (the margin above). The emitters that Remastered counts in whole milliseconds already get half a millisecond from the rounding, and Remastered itself gives them at least 33 ms.

**Crawling projectiles (`FixDamping`, `FixTimers`, `BulletPtdeSpeed`).** The undead dragons spit acid in volleys of globs that land and slide along the ground. While a glob slides, the game lands it again about three times a frame, and each landing keeps 0.97 of its speed and sets a ground factor that the next update multiplies by 0.4. Both counted per frame, so at a high frame rate the acid lost its speed several times faster and stopped short: standing in one spot, a player was hit at 30 and 60 FPS and missed at 120. With `FixDamping` both follow the frame time. A repeating emitter counts its interval down by the frame time and fires on the frame after the count runs out, so a volley comes up to a frame after its interval: the acid's 0.1 s interval gives a volley every 0.133 s at 30 FPS, and at a high frame rate that frame was short, so the volleys came faster. With `FixTimers` the count gives the native rate's period at any frame rate. Remastered applies the same per-frame values 60 times a second, so its acid loses its slide speed twice as fast as in the original game, and its 0.01 s acid stream fires every 1/30 s instead of every 1/15 s. `BulletPtdeSpeed` (default `true`) uses the original game's rates; `false` keeps Remastered's own at any frame rate.

**Effect timing (`FixSfxActions`, Prepare to Die Edition).** Some effects reset a part's clock and spawn from it on the next frame, reading how many to spawn from a curve at that clock. The undead dragons' acid spawns its part on the ground with a count that is 0 at 0 s and 1 at 0.033 s: one at 30 FPS, and none at 60 FPS or more, where the next frame comes sooner, so the acid never showed on the ground. While an effect action runs, its clock is rounded up to the next 1/30 s, as a 30 FPS frame gives it, and put back afterwards. At 30 FPS nothing changes. Nine of the game's effects have such a step, all of them that acid. Remastered's own effects were rebuilt without it.

**Swaying effects (`FixSfxSway`).** Two of the effect library's particle movements (its "Yurayura" sway and its partial follow) turn every particle by a random angle once a counter passes the effect's limit (5 in most effects, up to 45), and the counter went up once a frame. About 280 of the game's effects sway and 320 partially follow. At 240 FPS the particles turned eight times as often: the Blighttown spider offspring's magma tentacle ends in flames that dance on its body at 30 FPS and trailed far behind it at 240. The counter now counts the 30 FPS frames of the game's clock, so the particles turn as often as at 30 FPS at any frame rate. At 30 FPS nothing changes. The library's jittering effect follow (an effect attached to its owner that turns by a random angle every few frames) counts the same way and gets the same fix, though no effect in a tour of every area used it. In Remastered the counter counts the original game's 30 FPS frames with `SfxSwayPtdeSpeed`, else Remastered's 60. Which suits depends on whose effects are drawn (see "Effect looks in Remastered" below). Remastered rebuilt most of its swaying effects: 341 of the 396 effects with the sway have a different set of effect parts than the original's, and only 228 of its 1,934 swaying parts keep the original game's count and angle. Where it changed the count it mostly made it shorter (20 to 6, 10 to 2), so those effects were made to be seen turning at Remastered's 60 FPS, and `false` keeps that look. The Prepare to Die Edition effects mod puts the original game's effects back (all but 4 of the swaying parts with it installed), and those were made at 30 FPS: `true` suits them.

**Effect trails (`FixSfxTrails`).** Effects draw trails (ribbons through a particle's recent positions) behind sorceries, projectiles and some enemy attacks. A trail keeps its points for its lifetime (a third of a second in most effects), in a history whose size is fixed when the trail starts: the lifetime at 60 points a second, the rate the effects were made for. 112 of the game's 165 trail nodes add a point every frame, so above 60 FPS the history filled before the lifetime ended and the oldest points were dropped early: in a test at Firelink Shrine (2026-11-15) the trails covered 28% of their lifetime at 240 FPS and 21% at 347 FPS, and all of it at 30 and 60 FPS. A trail now takes a new point at most every 1/60 s when its history can then hold 90% of its lifetime, else every 1/30 s as at 30 FPS, while its head still follows the effect every frame. The steps are just under a whole 60 FPS frame (0.95/60 or 0.95/30 s), so no frame rate sits on their border; earlier test builds used the exact step the history needed, which for some trails fell on 1/60 s, and with it the trails covered all of their lifetime at 240 and 347 FPS in the same test. Up to 60 FPS the first kind takes a point every frame, as before; the second (80 of the 165 nodes: short trails and trails with few points) takes one every other frame at 60 FPS, as at 30, so it keeps its 30 FPS length. Remastered sizes its trails the same way and 260 of its 263 trail nodes take a point every frame, so it gets the same fix. Its artists remade the trails (263 nodes in 160 effects, against the original game's 165 in 115) and saw them at 60 FPS: `SfxTrailsPtdeSpeed` uses the original game's rule above (62 of Remastered's nodes then take a point every other frame at 60 FPS), and `false` gives a point at most every 1/60 s, so trails look as at Remastered's 60 FPS at any frame rate and nothing changes up to 60 FPS.

**Effect looks in Remastered (`SfxDistortionPtdeSpeed`, `SfxSwayPtdeSpeed`, `SfxTrailsPtdeSpeed`).** Three of the effect fixes follow a frame rate the effects were made for: heat haze steps its pattern on it, swaying particles turn on it, and trails take their points at it. The original game's effects were made at 30 FPS. Remastered's artists rebuilt most effects and made them at its 60 FPS (where they changed how often particles sway, they mostly made them sway more often). The Prepare to Die Edition effects mod for Remastered ("Dark Souls Remastered: PTDE Effects Fully Restored") puts the original game's effects back: with it installed, 1,787 of the 1,791 swaying effect parts and all of the trails are the original game's. Each switch takes `true` (the original game's look), `false` (Remastered's) or `auto`, the default: at startup the mod checks the 29 effect archives in the game's `sfx` folder against Remastered's own (by size), and when more than half are replaced (the effects mod replaces all 29, in every release so far) `auto` takes the original game's look, else Remastered's. A mod that changes a few effects is not taken for it. Installing or removing the effects mod, or verifying the game files in Steam, changes what `auto` picks at the next start, and the log says what it found (a line starting `Effects:`). If the archives come from somewhere else (a mod loader that redirects files), set the three switches by hand.

**Sprint graze (`FixGraze`).** The game slows a character that barely moved last frame (it assumes a wall). The test is per frame, so at a high frame rate ordinary running failed it. The fix applies the game's own test to real speed with no slack, and the slowdown and recovery per second match the native rate exactly. Earlier Remastered builds gave it some slack, which let a character climb a small step after a few seconds of jitter, which the game never allows.

**Ground snap and ladders (`FixStepDown`).** Every physics step the game lifts the character by its step height, moves it, then pulls it back down onto the ground, at most a fixed distance per step. At a high frame rate the same pull is applied many more times per second, which glued the character to curved lips and pulled it through the floor at the bottom of a ladder slide. The pull is now limited to the native rate per second: the pulls of the frames within the last native frame's time (1/30 s, or 1/60 s in Remastered) together stay within one native pull. Up to 1.7.0 that window held the last 8 frames, so above about 255 FPS (Remastered: 510, or 255 with `StepDownPtde`) it covered less than a native frame and the snap could pull the character down faster than the game does; since 1.8.0 it holds enough frames for any frame rate up to 1000. (Prepare to Die Edition called this `FixLadder`; that name still works.)

**Lips above a step (`FixStepUp`).** The same lift lets the character over anything up to its step height in one frame. A lip a little higher is climbed by creeping: each frame the lifted character rides a little way up the edge and the pull-down leaves it there, until it is far enough over to pop up on top. How far one frame's move gets decides it, so the shorter frames of a high frame rate crept far less: the Great Hollow's 0.37 m lip was climbed in 0.3-0.4 s at 30 FPS and never at 60, 120 or 240. Now a character that is pushing but hardly moving (over the last native frame it covered under 30% of the distance its speed asked for) is moved as at the native frame rate: its frames still run one by one, but at the end of each native frame's worth of time they are moved again as one move of that whole time, from where they started, which is exactly the native frame's move. That lip is now climbed in 0.27-0.38 s from 45 to 240 FPS (0.31-0.33 s at 30); a sharp-edged block above the step height that the native frame rate stops at stays unclimbable (a first version that raised the step height instead climbed faster than at 30 FPS and got over a wall corner that 30 FPS cannot); paths in the open and slides along walls are unchanged and smooth. At the native frame rate and below nothing changes.

**Walls lifting characters (`FixStepDown`).** The pull-down finds the ground with a probe cast down from just above the lifted character. When that probe starts inside a wall beside it, it reports ground at its own start, the lift is not taken back, and the character ends a step higher, every frame for as long as the wall is there: the Lower Undead Burg's door-ambush thief, pressed against the wall by its door, rose 1 m at 30 FPS but 12 to 16 m at 120. Real ground never gives that answer, so above the native frame rate such a frame takes the lift back as if no ground was found; at the native rate the game's own behaviour stays.

Only the pull counts against that limit: how far below its moved position the snap puts the character. Up to 1.7.0 the character's whole descent was counted, including its own movement down a slope. On a fast slide, such as the slopes in the Depths and before the Bed of Chaos, the slide's own speed could use up the limit. The character could then lose the ground when steered to the side, and the game treated it as airborne: its speed was clamped, the slide braked, and the slide sound stopped. 1.8.0 counts only the pull, as the game itself does at the native rate.

In Remastered, a ladder slide at about 30 FPS moved far enough in one frame to look like that lift had been skipped. The snap was then cut on every frame of the slide, and the character rose instead of sliding down. The lift is now read from the lifted position, so a real slide is left alone.

**Slide contact (`FixSlideContact`).** A steep slide records "feet on the slide" once a frame (bit 0 of a byte the game hands to the character's behaviour graph). At a high frame rate that record can miss for a single short frame at the lip, and the slide animation cancels. The game at its own rate is only ever as strict as one of its frames, so the record is kept until it has been missing for that long: 1/30 s of the original game, 1/60 s of Remastered (`SlidePtdeSpeed`). The hold is 95% of that so that frame-time jitter near the native rate cannot switch it on and off. At the native rate and below no frame is held, so the game there is exactly as before: with `SlidePtdeSpeed` off that is 60 FPS and below, with it on 30 FPS and below. At 120 FPS one frame is held (three with `SlidePtdeSpeed` on), at 240 FPS three (seven). A gap longer than a quarter of a second, such as a load, drops the record. The physics contact flags themselves are unchanged.

**Slide steering (`FixSlideSteer`).** Turning on a slide restarts the turn animation every frame, so the only live layer is one frame old. Havok scales the root motion by (total layer weight / 0.1) when that weight is under 0.1, and a layer's weight after t seconds is a smoothstep of t over its blend time, about 3 (t / blend time)^2 for a frame. So the body moves (frame time)^3 / 0.1 per frame and (frame time)^2 / 0.1 per second: the higher the frame rate, the less of the turn reaches the body (the legs play it, the body barely moves). While that root-motion query runs, the 0.1 threshold is multiplied by (frame time × rate) squared, where rate is 60 (30 with `SlidePtdeSpeed`), so the turn covers the same distance per second at any frame rate. Remastered kept the original game's blend times and runs at twice the rate, so out of the box its slide turn carries a quarter of the original game's weight at 60 FPS: with `SlidePtdeSpeed` on the threshold is a quarter at 60 FPS and the slide steers as in the original game; with it off, 60 FPS is left exactly as it is and other frame rates are matched to it. Below the rate the factor grows to at most 4 (30 FPS with the Remastered rate), because the weight stops growing as the square once a frame is a large part of the blend time. The stored threshold and the pose blending that uses it are unchanged.

**HUD bars (`FixUI`).** The health, stamina and boss bars move toward a new value by a fixed step every frame, so at a high frame rate an Estus heal was over in a few frames. The step now covers the same real time at any frame rate. The step is also the game's threshold for the yellow part of a bar: a change no bigger than one step is shown at once, and a bigger one holds the old value for a moment (1.5 s on the stamina bar) before it drains. A sprint takes one stamina point every 0.1 s, less than a step, so in the game it never leaves a yellow part. Before 1.8.0 the threshold was scaled with the step, so at a high frame rate each sprint point started that hold and a yellow part grew, vanished and came back for as long as the sprint lasted. Since 1.8.0 a step is scaled only while the bar is already moving, so the threshold is the game's own. The bonfire swirl on the loading screen also turns at the native speed.

**Sprint stamina drain (`FixStaminaTick`).** One point every 0.1 s, but the game rounded each tick up to a whole frame and dropped the remainder. The remainder is kept, so the drain is 10 points a second at any frame rate.

**Soul counter (`FixSoulCounter`).** The souls picked up are counted on the HUD by a step that grows with the amount still left, added once a frame. At a high frame rate a large pickup finished several times too soon. The step is multiplied by the frame time over 1/30 s with `HudPtdeSpeed` on (the original game's speed) or over 1/60 s with it off (Remastered's own, twice as fast), and the fraction carries to the next frame. The counter still stops on the real total. One update adds at most four of the game's steps, so a hitch does not skip the count ahead.

**In-engine cutscene camera (`FixCutsceneCamera`, Prepare to Die Edition).** The characters in a real-time cutscene already move every frame. The camera picked the nearest 30 FPS key and held it, so at 60 FPS it held for two frames and then jumped. Position and rotation are now blended by the amount the game already computed between those keys. A real cut (more than a metre, or more than half a radian) still snaps, and so does a pair of keys more than one and a half frames apart. Pre-rendered movies stay 30 FPS. The zoom track already blended and is unchanged. Nothing about this is sent to other players.

**Timers, camera easing and velocities (`FixTimers`, `FixSmoothing`, `FixVelocity`).** Every place in both executables that loads a frame-like constant (30, 60, 1/30, 1/60) was checked, and the matching code of the two games was compared: FromSoftware changed such a constant from 30 to 60 in Remastered only where it counts per frame, which points out the per-frame ones. These were still tied to one fixed frame:

- `FixTimers`: the weapon buff glow fading in and out, the blend from animation into ragdoll, the glow on dropped items, and (Remastered) a load queue countdown. Each advanced 1/30 s (PTDE) or 1/60 s (Remastered) per frame.
- `FixSmoothing`: the follow camera's pivot and look-at point move a fixed fraction of the remaining gap each frame. They now cover the same fraction per second at any frame rate.
- `FixVelocity`: the speed of moving sounds, which FMOD uses for the Doppler pitch shift, was worked out as one frame's movement times 30 (PTDE) or 60 (Remastered), so at 120 FPS the pitch shift was a quarter (PTDE) or half (Remastered) as strong as intended. It now divides by the real frame time. From 1.3.0 to 2.0.1 the ragdoll's pull toward its pose was treated the same way, and that was wrong: the ragdoll driver pushes a body's root toward its pose by 30 (Remastered: 60) times the distance every update, a pull per second and not one frame's movement. Toward a pose that moves smoothly it already gives the pose's own speed at any frame rate. Divided by the frame time, a pose that jumped (a body or an enemy loading in, a body held off its pose by the floor) was pulled 8 times as hard at 240 FPS, and a character standing on or against a body takes the body's speed as a moving floor or wall, so bodies and enemies near them could be flung up into a ceiling or through the floor. Since 2.1.0 the pull is the game's own (30, or 60 in Remastered) at and above the native rate, and divides by the frame time only below it, where the game's value would carry the body past its pose.

In PTDE the engine's 1/30 step is rewritten with the frame time every frame, so the few places that copy it once into a setting (the Havok world setup, an image filter, the input repeat intervals registered after a key rebind) now read a fixed 1/30 instead of whatever the frame time was at that moment.

**Script memory (`FixScriptMemory`).** The enemy AI and one other set of game scripts run in Lua, and FromSoftware turned off Lua's own clean-up of unused script memory. The game instead clears each set out on a timer: every half second for the AI, every second for the other. The scripts use up memory every frame they run, and at the native rate that timer meant a clean-up every 15 frames (PTDE) or 30 frames (Remastered). At 360 FPS a half second is 180 frames, so six times as much (Remastered) or twelve times as much (PTDE) built up between clean-ups. In a crowded area such as Blighttown or the Catacombs the AI's script memory ran out, the script that asked for more raised an error that nothing in the game catches, and the game closed itself. While closing, a background thread used a part of the game that had already been shut down, so the crash report shows a "pure virtual function call" and no `CRASH:` line. The 1.6.0 reports at 360 FPS were this. With the fix, each clean-up comes at least every 15 or 30 frames as at the native rate, and still every half second (or second) of real time at a lower frame rate. Each clean-up costs what it did at the native rate, and they come once per the same number of frames, so the share of frame time they take is the same as at the native rate.

**D-pad hold (`FixDpadHold`, Remastered).** Holding down on the D-pad returns to the first quick item after 15 frames, a quarter of a second at 60 FPS but an eighth at 120, so an ordinary tap could trigger it. The hold is timed in real time: a quarter of a second at any frame rate, including below 60 FPS.

**Kick and jumping attack (`FixStickFlick`, since 2.1.0).** A kick is R1 and a jumping attack R2 together with a forward "flick" of the left stick. The game takes a push as a flick when the stick was neutral (every direction at most 0.2) on the previous frame and is past 0.3 now, and shows the flick for 0.06 s; the attack only becomes a kick or a jumping attack if the button press is seen while the flick is shown. Both tests count frames. At a high frame rate the stick is read between 0.2 and 0.3 on its way out of neutral, so the flick never starts: with a quick push (neutral to full in 50 ms) the original game kicks 84% of the time at 30 FPS, 32% at 144 and never at 240. And a press is seen on the frame after it happens: at 30 FPS a press up to a frame before the stick reached 0.3 still came with the flick, at 240 FPS it came 4 ms early and was a plain attack. The fix keeps the game's rules but times them on the native frames in real time: the neutral test looks at the stick one native frame before the native frame that first saw it past 0.3, the flick is shown until the end of the native frame the game would last show it in, and a fresh R1 or R2 press made while the stick is on its way out of neutral toward forward is held back until the flick starts or that native frame ends (at most 1/30 s; a press with the stick at rest or already held is never held back). Measured against the original game's own code, the chance of a kick for a given push and press timing at 144, 240 or 1000 FPS is that of 30 FPS, and at 30 FPS and below nothing changes. Remastered uses the same code at 60 FPS, so it already needed a push twice as quick as the original game; with `StickInputPtdeSpeed` (on by default; `StickFlickPtdeSpeed` is accepted as another name) it gets the original game's 30 FPS timing, with it off Remastered's own 60 FPS timing at any frame rate.

**Roll direction (`FixRollDirection`, since 2.1.0).** The roll goes the way the stick was pushed, even when it was let go a moment before the roll button: the game keeps the stick of its last frames in a ring of 6 entries and, when the button is let go, takes the strongest push of the newest 5 (each frame back counts 0.9 times as much). With nothing there the character backsteps (if the stick was neutral when the button went down) or does nothing. That memory is 0.13 s at 30 FPS, 0.067 s at Remastered's 60 and 0.017 s at 240, so at a high frame rate a push let go of just before the roll button came out as a backstep instead of a roll that way. The fix reads the same strongest push over the native rate's last 4 frames of real time, each native frame back counting 0.9 times as much; at the native rate and below it is the game's own reading of its own ring. `StickInputPtdeSpeed` (Remastered, on by default) uses the original game's 30 FPS frames, off Remastered's own 60 FPS frames.

**Switchback (`FixSwitchback`, since 2.1.0).** Reversing the stick while running or sprinting plays a quick turn-around animation when the stick's direction swings more than 105 degrees since the previous frame (the turn animation keeps the last 2 frames; the debug menu calls the 2 its reference frames). That frame is 1/30 s in the original game, so a reversal over up to about 50 ms usually played it; at 240 FPS a frame is 4 ms and only an instant flip did. Of 180-degree reversals over 40 ms, 77% played it at 30 FPS, none at 240. The fix measures the swing on the native frames in real time: from the stick's direction at the native frame before the one the game would look at next, at most once per native frame, as the game looks once a frame. The same reversals now play it 80% of the time at 144, 240 and 1000 FPS, and a reversal never plays it twice. At the native rate and below it is the game's own test. `StickInputPtdeSpeed` (Remastered, on by default) uses the original game's 30 FPS frames, off Remastered's own 60.

**Status build-up from the ground (`FixSpEffectTiming`, since 2.1.0).** The game counts a special effect's time down by the frame time, notices on the next update that it ran out, and applies the effect that follows it one update later. An effect that is already active cannot be applied again, so one that something applies every frame comes back on the update that finds the old one run out. Blighttown's swamp floor applies a 0.06 s effect every frame whose follow-up takes 3 points off the poison resistance: one round took 3 frames at 30 FPS (30 points a second), 5 at 60 (36), 16 at 240 (45) and 61 at 1000 (48), so the swamp poisoned up to 60% faster. Every short effect that is kept up this way, and every chain of effects, is timed like this. With the fix an effect is given the time that makes it run out on the update the native frame rate would, to the nearest frame, so the swamp poisons 30 points a second at any frame rate (Remastered: 36 at its own 60 FPS, or 30 with `SpEffectPtdeSpeed`, on by default). Longer effects change by a fraction of a frame. At the native rate and below nothing changes.

**Effects that act at intervals (`FixSpEffectTiming`, since 2.3.0).** An effect with an interval (lava's 125 points every 0.1 s, heals over time such as 7 s of 10 points every 0.1 s, the 1 s and 3 s ones) acts on the update that finds its interval timer at or below zero, then sets the timer back to the interval rather than adding it. So each wait is rounded up to whole frames: in the original game, which stepped exactly 1/30 s a frame, 0.1 s took 3 frames and 0.2 s took 7 (float rounding). At other frame rates the rounding differs: 0.1 s acted 14% less often at 60 FPS, 0.2 s up to 17% more often, 0.5 s 5-7% more; and with this mod at 30 FPS, whose frames last the real time and so a little more or less than 1/30 s, half of the 0.1 s waits took a fourth frame (15% less often). With the fix the timer is set to the original game's period minus how late the effect acted, so it acts on the original rhythm at any frame rate, 30 included. Measured on an effect of 6 points every 0.1 s (the original's 60 points a second): 49, 55 and 58 points a second at 30, 60 and 240 FPS without the fix, 60 with it at all three (Remastered: its own 60 FPS rhythm, or the original's with `SpEffectPtdeSpeed`).

**Lingering attacks (`FixBulletHitTiming`, since 2.1.0).** A projectile that can hit the same target more than once keeps a record of whom it hit, and leaves each of them alone for its re-hit time (BulletParam `dmgHitRecordLifeTime`, 0.1 to 2 s): 0.2 s for the clouds of Poison Mist and Toxic Mist, 0.4 s for Acid Surge, 0.5 s for the Vow of Silence and Tranquil Walk of Peace areas, 1.5 s for Chaos Storm, 2 s for Great Chaos Fireball's lava, and 0.1 to 2 s for a number of enemy attacks. The game counts that time down by the frame time, so it lasts whole frames: in the original game's fixed 1/30 s steps a 0.2 s re-hit time takes 7 frames (0.233 s, as the float steps add up just short of 0.2), 0.15 s takes 5 and 0.5 s takes 16. At a high frame rate the same times last about what they say, so a target standing in such an area was hit 17% more often (0.2 s: every hit of Poison Mist adds 10 to poison build-up, of Toxic Mist to toxic, so both built up that much faster), 11% (0.15 s and 0.3 s), 8% (0.4 s), 7% (0.5 s) or 2% (1.5 to 2 s). With the fix a new record entry gets the time that makes it run out after the native frames the game would count, to the nearest frame: a 0.2 s cloud hits 4.3 times a second at 60, 144, 240 and 1000 FPS, as in the original game. Remastered follows `BulletPtdeSpeed` (on by default: the original game's 30 FPS frames; off: Remastered's own 60). At the native rate and below nothing changes.

**Scripted sequences (`FixEventWaits`, since 2.4.0).** Every wait in the game's event scripts ("wait N seconds", "wait N frames" and the random waits: a trap's rhythm, a door, an enemy's scripted attack, a boss's entrance) keeps its remaining time and counts it down by the frame time, done when it reaches zero. The original game stepped exactly 1/30 s a frame, so a wait lasts whole frames: rounding leaves 0.5 s needing 16 of them (0.533 s) and 2 s 61 (2.033 s), and 121 of the scripts' 193 timed waits end a frame after their nominal time. At a high frame rate the same waits ended up to a frame of the original (33 ms) sooner, and a long one drifted (a 600 s wait lasted 600.67 s at 150 FPS). Mostly that cannot be seen, but where a script times an attack against something moving it can: the Undead Burg's barrel thrower pushes his barrel, and two half-second waits later the barrel's damaging part comes; at 150 FPS it came 0.054 s sooner, while the barrel was still 2.5 cm inside his reach, and he was hit by it (measured in game). With the fix each wait ends on the frame nearest to where the game's own countdown ends at the native rate, worked out with the game's own float arithmetic; at the native rate it ends on the original's frame. Checked for every wait from 0 to 600 s at 30 to 1000 FPS, with even and uneven frame times. Remastered follows `EventWaitPtdeSpeed` (on by default: the original game's 30 FPS; off: Remastered's own 60, where most waits last their nominal time).

**Render thread (Prepare to Die Edition, since 2.2.0).** In busy places (Blighttown, Darkroot Garden, the Undead Burg) the frame rate is limited by the game's draw thread, which spends most of its time on its own overhead rather than on drawing. Each of these switches takes part of that work away without changing a pixel or a game value, and each can be turned off: `AsyncReadback` (once a frame the game copies a small render target back to the CPU and waits for the GPU to finish everything before it; the copy is now taken from two frames earlier, which is ready), `FilterSamplerStates` (about 17,000 sampler state calls a frame, nearly all repeating what is already set, are answered without reaching Direct3D; since 2.6.0 also the filter states that `AnisotropicFiltering` upgrades, when they would leave the device as it is: 3% more frames in the Demon Ruins with 16x), `FastEffects` (the effects library was linked as a debug build: its stack fills and checks are skipped, and since 2.3.0 its busiest routines, the particles' keyframe curves (the eased ones too, which the Kiln of the First Flame's ash leans on: on the render thread, whose x87 precision is a float's, in single-precision SSE with the same results), colour packing, sine/cosine polynomial, matrix glue, vector-array passes and point bounds, and the per-particle loops of the particle update and quad builder, run as direct code doing the same operations in the same order, checked against the game's own code including at each x87 precision: in Quelaag's Domain 151 to 180 FPS before the two loops, which add another 3%; with the bone and box work, 138.5 to 179.5 FPS in the heaviest view there), `FastRenderMath` (28 small vector, matrix and conversion helpers replaced with SSE versions that give bit-for-bit the same results, checked against the game's own code on 400,000 cases; since 2.3.0 also the code that hands animated models' bone matrices to the shaders, which went through a chain of calls for every bone of every draw, and the code that finds the changed shader constants, which tested them one bit at a time: the same memory writes and the same Direct3D calls in the same order, checked call by call, and 142 to 148.5 FPS in Quelaag's Domain, where the animated egg sacs fill the view; and the bounding-box transform every model goes through, in packed SSE with the same results; and the runtime's square root, which on the render thread went through the C runtime's floating-point error handling for every call because of Direct3D's x87 precision setting, done directly with the same result), `FastReadLocks` (the game's read locks, taken hundreds of times a frame, count readers without calls into the Windows kernel, under the same rules), `FastMutexes` (since 2.6.0: the game's mutexes, taken about 200 times a frame by the game thread alone, each time a call into the Windows kernel to take and another to give back, about 1.7 us a pair, are taken and given back with one interlocked instruction, about 5 ns, under the same rules: the thread holding one can take it again, a thread that has to wait sleeps. A kernel mutex also hands itself to the thread that waited first, so the game thread queued behind threads that still had to be woken: in a heavy Blighttown view (2026-11-17) one frame in twenty took 7.5 to 10 ms where the others took 2.6. With it, at 4K: the frames' 95th percentile from 7.6 to 3.3 ms and the slowest from 10 to 3.6 ms, at the same average; at 1280x720, where the game thread is the limit, 345 to 481 FPS) and `ReuseSurfaces` (five depth buffers and a small surface the game creates and throws away every frame are kept and handed out again; this matters most under DXVK). With all of them on a Ryzen 7 5800X3D and an RTX 3090 at 4K went from 79 to 157-171 FPS in Darkroot's busiest spot, 58-73 to 96 in Blighttown (129 with DXVK) and 45-125 to 155-168 on the Undead Burg bridge. `FilterShaderConstants` (the same filter for vertex shader constants) is off by default: in the game under 1% of them repeat. 2.2.0 also reads an unplugged controller slot once a second instead of every frame (each read of an empty slot costs about 0.4 ms), which made the game slower while standing still with fewer than four pads. 2.4.0 takes more of the same work away: a render target the size of the scene that the game made and threw away every frame is kept (`ReuseSurfaces`), the game's own sampler-state wrappers answer repeats before they reach Direct3D (`FilterSamplerStates`), and `FastRenderMath` keeps its matrix paths in registers. `RenderMathPrecision` decides how exactly the render thread's math keeps the original arithmetic: `exact` gives every result identical to the original game's; `relaxed` (the default) lets render-only math (matrix multiplies and multiply-adds, the engine's sine and cosine, the effects' particle loops and keyframe curves) round once in float instead of through double, as a modern compiler would build it. About a quarter of those results differ in the last binary digit, far below what a pixel can show, and nothing the render thread works out goes back into the game: physics, AI and hits are always exact. Together, in seven views measured where the draw thread is the limit (1280x720 window, Ryzen 7 5800X3D, RTX 3090): 4 to 6.5% more frames (Quelaag's Domain 179 to 190 FPS, Blighttown 265 to 282, the Demon Ruins 192 to 204), and 3 to 4% less render work again where effects fill the view.

**Bonfire softlock (`BonfireFix` on Remastered, `BonfireUnstick` on Prepare to Die Edition).** After resting, the bonfire menu can disappear while the character stays seated, and nothing responds until the game is quit. If one of the three idle sits lasts a second with no menu open, the mod tells the character to stand. After the Reverse Hollowing or Kindle prompt it waits until 8 seconds after the prompt (Remastered: after any menu), since those two play about 6 seconds with the menu closed, so the mod never stands the character up in the middle of one. Prepare to Die Edition watches the fields published with FPSFix+. Remastered watches the 2022 executable's own menu-open fields and writes the local player's seated animation id. Both are on by default, including when the key is missing from an older INI. Remastered also accepts the name `BonfireUnstick`.

## FPS Unlock Settings

An in-game page with the settings you may want to change while playing: press **F1** (`SettingsMenuKey`, F1 to F12 or none), or **Y** on a controller in the game's PC settings screen (a line at the bottom of that screen says so). Up/down chooses a row, left/right changes it, LB/RB (or Q/E) turns the page, B or Esc closes it; while it is open the game gets no controller, keyboard or mouse input.

- **Display**: the frame rate cap, the fullscreen refresh rate, borderless fullscreen, the HUD size and the size of the marks over the world, the mouse pointer.
- **Picture**: depth of field strength, anti-aliasing (off, 2x, 4x, 8x or the game's option; off and on at once), smooth foliage edges, texture filtering, shadow detail, supersampling, colour precision, 10-bit output, swaying foliage, NPCs' feet on the ground.
- **Draw distance**: map pieces (the game's, adjacent, adjacent+, all), the objects of nearby rooms, the detail and effect distances.
- **Co-op** (Prepare to Co-op Edition): the password (typed on the keyboard, shown as dots), players in a session, invaders, the invasion cooldown, the health bars over players, players' feet on the ground, Crimson Blossoms, Bloody Mary, enemies scaled by nearby players, network updates, shared drops.

Each change is written to the INI at once (only the value on that setting's line changes). The page says when a row applies: at once (the frame rate cap, the HUD size, depth of field, smooth foliage edges, the draw distance rows), after a restart of the game (the rest), or, for the co-op rows, at once when no session runs and otherwise when the current one ends. The fixes, speed-ups and diagnostics stay in the INI only. `SettingsMenu = false` turns the page off.


**Remastered** has the same page (F1, or Y in the game's PC settings screen; the same controls), with its own rows:

- **Frame rate**: the frame rate cap (at once), real time when the frame rate dips (`VariableFrameTime`, at once), menu presses counted once, standing up from a stuck bonfire, starting offline, skipping the start-up logos.
- **Movement**: for each movement switch, the original game's feel or Remastered's: lock-on turn speed, sliding off a slope, walls and corners, ground that drops away, step height, momentum in the air, feet on stairs and slopes, slides, stick timing.
- **Timing**: the same for the HUD bars, poison and other effects, scripted waits, crawling and repeating attacks, heat haze, swaying particles and trails (the last three also Auto: the original game's with the PTDE effects mod installed).

All of them apply at once except menu presses, the bonfire, stick timing, heat haze and trails (after a restart) and starting offline and the logos (at the next start). Changing the cap while the game runs keeps the frame pacer's clock continuous (before, a lower cap set the clock back by every frame the higher one had run ahead, and the game waited that long). The page is drawn in the swap chain's Present with Direct3D 11 (every piece of state it touches is put back); the controller is read through the game's own XInput import, the keyboard through its DirectInput keyboard and its user32 key reads, and while the page is open the game gets none of them.

## Prepare to Die Edition and DSfix

[DSfix](https://github.com/PeterTh/dsfix) by Peter "Durante" Thoman is a legendary mod, for Dark Souls and for PC modding as a whole: for years it was how Prepare to Die Edition was played on PC, and earlier versions of this mod expected it for the best experience. From 2.3.0 this mod does not need it. DSfix stays fully supported; it is now optional.

Where this README compares this mod with DSfix, it means Durante's DSfix 2.x (2.4, its last release). The comparisons do not refer to DSfix 3.0 by DDTM, a separate continuation with changes of its own.

**Why a new approach.** DSfix solved high resolution, depth of field and anti-aliasing for this game more than a decade ago, and there is no reason to assume that its way is the best or the only one. This mod handles the same problems its own way, with one goal: faithfulness. The game should look the way it was made to look, at a higher quality and a higher frame rate, with nothing lost on the way (the speed-ups give the same picture, checked bit for bit; the one difference in principle is the eye adaptation starting two frames later, `AsyncReadback`). Where it differs from DSfix 2.x:

- **Colour above 8 bits per channel** (`HighPrecisionColor`): DSfix's post-processing works on the game's 8-bit scene.
- **A depth of field measured against the original**, as strong as the game's at any resolution and without blocks (`DepthOfFieldResolution`). In our comparisons (DSfix 2.x at 3840x2160 with `dofOverrideResolution 1080` and `dofBlurAmount 1`) its depth of field blurred less than the original; DSfix's `dofBlurAmount` adds blur by hand.
- **Glows, mist, fog and smoke at a matching resolution** with the game's own depth-aware upsample (`EffectsResolution`), and bloom, glare and the depth of field's sources made from every pixel of the scene.
- **The sun and moon**: rendered above 1024x720 with DSfix 2.x's resolution, they stayed hidden after something passed in front of them. This mod's resolution never has the problem (`FixSun` repairs it for DSfix 2.x's resolution too; DSfix 3.0 corrects it itself, and `FixSun` then leaves it alone).

**Using DSfix.** This mod loads beside it (this mod is `xinput1_3.dll`, DSfix `DINPUT8.dll`) and drives the frame rate: set `unlockFPS 0` in `DSfix.ini`. Both mods have picture settings (resolution, depth of field, texture filtering, HUD scale), and `YieldToDSFix` decides whose apply, so the two never both change the same thing:

- **`YieldToDSFix = false` (the default): this mod's picture.** Leave it off when DSfix is there for its other features (DSfix 3.0's quality of life, for example) and this mod should make the picture: its resolution, depth of field, effects, water, HUD scale and speed-ups. They take precedence over DSfix's by themselves (DSfix's `renderWidth` and `renderHeight` are then left unused, for example), with nothing to change in `DSfix.ini`.
- **`YieldToDSFix = true`: DSfix's picture.** Set it only to use DSfix's own picture settings: its resolution, anti-aliasing, SSAO, depth of field, texture filtering, and HUD and menu scale. `RenderResolution`, `Supersampling`, `DepthOfFieldStrength`, `DepthOfFieldResolution`, `EffectsResolution`, `WaterResolution`, `HighPrecisionColor` and `AnisotropicFiltering` then do nothing, `ShadowMapResolution` leaves the game's own 2048 shadow map, and so do the HUD and menu settings (`UIScale`, `UIScaleWorld`, `UIScaleText`, `UIScaleMenus` and `FixBossBarOverlap`) and `FixAspect` and `FixAspectUI` (a screen other than 16:9 keeps the game's 16:9 picture): the game draws its HUD and menus as it does without this mod, which is where DSfix 3.0's HUD mod and `weaponWearBars` look for them. `FullscreenRefreshRate` leaves the fullscreen refresh rate to DSfix's `fullscreenHz` (this mod's hook sits beneath DSfix's and would otherwise replace the rate DSfix chose). DSfix's resolution needs the game's own anti-aliasing off: with it on, the 3D picture fills only a corner of the screen. This mod keeps it off while DSfix makes the resolution, whatever the game's Antialiasing option says (up to 3.0.0 the option had to be turned off by hand); DSfix's own anti-aliasing (`aaType`) smooths the picture then.

Either way the rest of this mod stays on: the frame rate unlock and every fix, the speed-ups, the borderless options, `AlphaToCoverage`, `FixSun` and `FixWater` (off beside DSfix 3.0, which corrects the water itself), and with `YieldToDSFix = false` `FullscreenRefreshRate`, `FixAspect`, `FixAspectUI` and `ShadowMapResolution` (beside DSfix 3.0, which does not pass on the clipping rectangles of the larger map's outer slices to Direct3D, this mod puts them back beneath it; before 2.7.0 shadows further ahead then appeared only up close, at a hard line). Two settings are off whenever DSfix is installed, whatever `YieldToDSFix` says, because DSfix reads the game's depth and textures its own way: `FixAntialiasingDepth` and `SkipUnusedReflection`. `HighPrecisionColor` (colour above 8 bits) stays on beside DSfix unless DSfix's SMAA (`aaQuality` above 0 with `aaType SMAA`; DSfix's defaults are 0 and FXAA) runs on this mod's picture. It does only when this mod's resolution is the same size as `DSfix.ini`'s `renderWidth` x `renderHeight`, or is 1024x720, which DSfix makes its render size: SMAA's last pass reads the scene with the sRGB decoding only an 8-bit picture has, and on a 10-bit one it applied the gamma twice (a washed-out picture more than twice as bright, Firelink with DSfix 3.0, 2026-11-17). DSfix's FXAA and SSAO work on the 10-bit picture, and at DSfix 3.0's default size (1920x1080) beside this mod's 4K, DSfix's anti-aliasing and SSAO do not run at all (they look for the scene at DSfix's own size). Without DSfix installed, `YieldToDSFix` does nothing.

**Reports welcome.** If you play with DSfix, 2.x or 3.0, reports and comparisons with screenshots are welcome.

### DSfix 3.0

[DSfix 3.0](https://www.nexusmods.com/darksouls/mods/1985) by DDTM continues DSfix with quality-of-life features, controls and graphics of its own: keyboard and mouse together with a controller (DDTM Controller Fix), item sorting, a bonfire map, using many items or covenant offerings at once, changing covenant at a bonfire, quick save and load, HUD, camera and sharpening options, and more. This mod leaves quality of life to DSfix 3.0 and sticks to the frame rate, its fixes, performance and the picture, and we recommend DSfix 3.0 for those features. Its page recommends this mod in turn, with a guide for using both. There are two ways to combine them, depending on which mod should make the picture:

**1. DSfix 3.0 for quality of life, this mod for everything else.** This mod makes the picture (resolution, depth of field, effects, water, HUD scale) and runs the frame rate, the fixes and the speed-ups. `YieldToDSFix` is not needed: leave it at `false`. Leave `FixSun` on too: beside this mod's resolution it undoes DSfix 3.0's own sun correction, which would leave the sun and moon faint. The HUD and menu sizes are then this mod's (`UIScale`, `UIScaleMenus`): leave DSfix's `enableHudMod` at 0, as DSfix 3.0 ships, or both mods scale them. DSfix 3.0's `weaponWearBars` looks for the weapon slots at the game's own places, so with `UIScale` other than 100 it may not find them.

```ini
# DSfix.ini
unlockFPS 0

# PTDE-FPS-Unlock.ini
FixController = false
HideMouseCursor = false
```

**2. DSfix 3.0 for quality of life and for the picture.** DSfix 3.0 decides the resolution (its own scaling), anti-aliasing, depth of field, HUD and menu scale, the fullscreen refresh rate (`fullscreenHz`) and its other picture settings, and fixes the sun and moon and the water itself. On a screen other than 16:9 the picture keeps the game's 16:9 shape (`FixAspect` steps back, so DSfix's HUD mod finds the HUD). Turn `YieldToDSFix` on so that this mod's picture settings step back and the two do not fight:

```ini
# DSfix.ini
unlockFPS 0

# PTDE-FPS-Unlock.ini
YieldToDSFix = true
FixSun = false
FixController = false
HideMouseCursor = false
```

In both, DSfix 3.0's controls look after the controller and the mouse pointer (so `FixController` and `HideMouseCursor` are off), and the frame rate unlock, every fix and the speed-ups stay this mod's.

## Install

Copy the files from the release for your game next to the game's exe (see the table above; PTDE's `dxvk_d3d9.dll` is DXVK, which `DXVK = true` uses). To uninstall, delete them. `FPSUnlock` is the master switch: `false` turns every part of the mod off and leaves the game unchanged, except `SkipIntro` and Remastered's `StartOffline`, which have their own switches.

**Remastered.** The game imports `dinput8.dll`, so that file name has to stay. Another mod that also installs `dinput8.dll` needs a chain loader before the two can load together. In `System -> PC Settings -> Display`, set Frequency to the monitor refresh rate and Vertical sync to off, then restart the game after changing `MaxFPS`.

**PTDE: the INI.** `PTDE-FPS-Unlock.ini` starts with the settings you may want to change (`[Display]`: the frame rate cap, the refresh rate, borderless, the 16:10 fill, the intro; `[DXVK]`). The fixes and speed-ups follow below a line: they are on as the mod is meant to be played, and are there to compare with the original game or to troubleshoot. Every setting is read wherever it stands, so an INI from an earlier version keeps working.

**PTDE.** The mod loads as `xinput1_3.dll` so it can sit beside DSfix. In exclusive fullscreen it can ask for your display's highest refresh rate instead of the 60 Hz the game offers (`FullscreenRefreshRate`; with `YieldToDSFix = true` beside DSfix, DSfix's `fullscreenHz` decides), and it has a borderless mode (`BorderlessFullscreen`; set the game to windowed first): `auto`, the default, makes the window borderless only while the game runs on DXVK, where borderless keeps HDR, variable refresh and even pacing, and leaves it to the game with its own Direct3D 9, where exclusive fullscreen is better. With Windows display scaling above 100%, `DpiAware` (on by default) has the game draw at the monitor's real pixels; otherwise Windows has it draw a smaller picture and stretches it up, which blurs it and, in borderless, rules out G-SYNC / FreeSync.

**PTDE: DSfix.** Supported and optional: see [Prepare to Die Edition and DSfix](#prepare-to-die-edition-and-dsfix) (set `unlockFPS 0` in `DSfix.ini`; `YieldToDSFix = true` lets DSfix decide the picture settings it has). For quality-of-life features, use [DSfix 3.0](#dsfix-30) with the settings there.

**PTDE: DXVK.** `DXVK = true` (the default since 2.3.0) runs the game's Direct3D 9 on Vulkan through [DXVK](https://github.com/doitsujin/dxvk) (version 3.1.1, included as `dxvk_d3d9.dll`; keep it next to `xinput1_3.dll`). It helps most where the draw thread limits the frame rate (Blighttown: 96 FPS on Direct3D 9, 129 with DXVK on the PC above); where the GPU is the limit the difference can be small. DSfix keeps working on top of it. **HDR and G-SYNC / FreeSync under DXVK:** DXVK draws with Vulkan, but by default (`DxvkPresentThroughDxgi = true`) the mod shows its frames through DXGI, the way Direct3D 11 and 12 games do: Windows HDR stays on with the same picture as Direct3D 9, and variable refresh works in fullscreen and borderless windows alike. With `DxvkPacedPresent` (the default), each frame is flipped a steady delay after the moment the game made it (about a frame), so the frames reach the display spaced exactly like the game's time steps: DXVK's threads and the GPU finish frames unevenly, and flipped when done they went out 1-2 ms early or late, which showed as the refresh rate wandering and uneven camera motion. `EvenFrameSteps` (the default) makes those steps even: while the game keeps to `MaxFPS`, every frame advances the game by exactly the cap's step (the time measured on the draw thread also carried how long each game update took, a millisecond or two either side). On an AW3225QF (240 Hz, HDR, G-SYNC) in Quelaag's Domain at a 120 cap, the display then refreshed as evenly as on Direct3D 9 (spread 0.47-0.68 ms against 0.51 ms). **For the best variable refresh, set `MaxFPS` to a frame rate your PC holds in most areas** (watch a busy place) and below the display's refresh rate: where the game cannot keep up, its frames come unevenly again. (A Vulkan swap chain on Windows only gets variable refresh in exclusive fullscreen, and there Windows switches HDR off while the game runs.) It needs a driver that shares textures between Direct3D 11 and Vulkan, which current NVIDIA, AMD and Intel drivers do; otherwise the log says so and DXVK shows its frames itself. With `DxvkPresentThroughDxgi = false`, DXVK presents through Vulkan and `DxvkExclusiveFullscreen = auto` (the default) takes exclusive fullscreen, and with it variable refresh, only while HDR is off. **NVIDIA with Windows HDR on:** if RTX HDR is on for the game in the NVIDIA app, it applies only under DXVK, never under Direct3D 9, and changes the picture a lot (crushed shadows, brighter highlights, other colors); turn it off for the game for the same picture as Direct3D 9. The log says when it is active. It needs a graphics driver with Vulkan 1.3. If DXVK cannot start, the game runs on Direct3D 9 as usual and the log says why. The first time an area loads, DXVK compiles its shaders, which can cause brief hitches; they are cached for later runs. DXVK writes its own log, `DARKSOULS_d3d9.log`, next to the game, and reads an optional `dxvk.conf` from there. If the game folder already has a `d3d9.dll` (DXVK or ReShade installed by hand), that one stays in charge and `DXVK` does nothing. On the Steam Deck and Linux, Proton already runs the game on its own DXVK, so `DXVK` does nothing there either. With `DXVK = false` or `FPSUnlock = false`, DXVK is not loaded at all.

`MaxFPS` defaults to 120 in both and accepts 10 to 1000; a value outside that range is clamped to the nearest end. Pick what your PC can usually hold; dips below it do not slow the game. A low cap works too: both games run in real time at any frame rate down to 10 FPS, so a slower PC can cap at 30 for steady frame pacing. (In Remastered before 1.4.0 a cap below 60 broke the frame pacer, which then waited longer every frame; it now holds any cap exactly. Remastered also no longer drops a frame when a frame comes in a little late, which made a frame rate held below the cap, or a cap set outside the game, hitch.) (Frames longer than 1/8 s, below 8 FPS, are each treated as 1/8 s, so a hitch or a loading stall does not become one huge step.)

**PTDE: where the cap waits (`FPSLimitPoint`).** `early` (the default) waits at the start of each frame, before the game reads the controller, keyboard and mouse: the least input lag. `present` waits on the draw thread right before the frame is shown, gameplay and loading screens alike, after that input has been read: the most even frame timing, with slightly more input lag. Both keep the same schedule, `MaxFPS` stays the rate, and only the named point waits. A change of rate in game (Trace's keys) is taken safely, and the game is never left uncapped: if the `present` hooks cannot be placed, the cap waits at the frame boundary as 1.8.0 did. Restart the game after changing it.

**PTDE on phones (Android with GameNative or Winlator).** There the game's x86 code runs translated for the phone's ARM CPU, and a phone heats up and slows down within minutes at full speed. With `PhoneProfile = auto` (the default) the INI's `[Phones]` settings replace the ones above them only when the game runs translated; a PC is never affected, and the log says when they apply. They cap the game at 40 FPS (`PhoneMaxFPS`; GameNative shows at most 60) and keep the game's own 1024x720 scene (`PhoneRenderResolution = off`), which leaves the graphics chip cooler. Any setting can get a phone value the same way: its name with `Phone` in front. On a Snapdragon 888 phone with GameNative (Turnip driver v26.3.0, DXVK async-1.10.3, FEX preset Intermediate, 1280x720, present mode mailbox), Firelink held 40 FPS for a whole 12-minute test from a cool start, where the earlier setup slowed to 37 FPS after 7 minutes. GameNative's DXVK 2.x versions were 10-17% slower there and 3.1.1 did not reach the game; `DXVK = true` does nothing under Wine, where GameNative's own DXVK runs the game.

## Notes and known limits

- **Remastered lock-on speed.** Remastered pans the lock-on camera and turns the body toward the target twice as fast as the original (it kept the original game's per-frame values at 60 FPS). The INI included with 1.5.0 sets `LockOnPtdeSpeed = true`, which uses the original speed for both (the old name `CameraPtdeSpeed` still works). Set it to `false` for Remastered's own speed.
- **Short steps after riding the edge (PTDE).** Walking along a short step at a sharp angle can leave the character riding its edge. At 30 FPS turning into the step then crosses it; at a high frame rate the character can get stuck against it like a low wall. The game decides step-ups once per physics step, and stepping the physics every frame changes the outcome on this borderline case. Running the player's physics at a fixed 30 steps a second does fix it, but needs interpolation that adds visible delay, so it is not included.
- **PTDE cutscenes.** Character animation in an in-engine cutscene already advances every frame. `FixCutsceneCamera` blends the camera between its 30 FPS keys; a hard cut still snaps. Pre-rendered movies are 30 FPS by nature.
- **Camera speeding up (Remastered, reports with 1.2.0).** Players reported the free camera suddenly speeding up, especially when running at an angle to it. Remastered's camera already swings toward your running direction faster than the original game's (it kept the original per-frame values at 60 FPS), and in 1.2.0 the blend between the camera's follow settings (how tightly the pivot and the look-at point follow your character) still ran once per frame, so at a high frame rate those changes finished two to four times sooner. 1.3.0 fixed that blend (`FixSmoothing`). 1.5.0 also scales the extra follow that lasts for two seconds after you turn the camera with the stick or mouse (`FixCamera`); without it, that part still finished several times too fast above 60 FPS, including just after locking on. If the camera still misbehaves, send the log: it has a mouse line once a minute while the mouse moves. `LockOnPtdeSpeed = true` (on in the 1.5.0 INI) also makes the camera settle at the original game's slower speed.
- **First walk-to-run (Remastered).** The retail game has a short hitch on the first walk-to-run after standing still. At a high frame rate it reads as a brief freeze of the character and camera; it is left as it is.
- **Bonfire softlock.** After rest the menu can close while the character stays seated and nothing responds. `BonfireFix` (Remastered) and `BonfireUnstick` (Prepare to Die Edition) are on by default. Each tells the character to stand once it has sat for a second with no menu open, and after the Reverse Hollowing or Kindle prompt not before 8 seconds after it (Remastered: after any menu), as those take about 6 seconds with the menu closed. The log line starts with `Bonfire softlock:`. If the character stays seated and that line never appears, send the log.
- **Very high frame rates.** 240 FPS is not sustainable on every PC. Below the cap the game still plays in real time.
- **Very low frame rates.** Down to 10 FPS the game keeps real time, but each physics step is long (six times a 60 FPS step at 10 FPS). The game pulls a character down onto the ground by at most a fixed distance per step, so around 10 to 15 FPS a character sprinting down steep stairs or slopes can briefly leave the ground. This is the same rule that makes the original 30 FPS game slightly looser than Remastered's 60.
- **Weapon durability and hit windows.** Worth checking if you depend on exact timing.

## Logs and bug reports

Each mod writes a log next to its DLL (`DSR-FPS-Unlock.log` or `PTDE-FPS-Unlock.log`) with a heartbeat every 30 seconds. The first lines show the version, the exe check and each patch applied. With `DXVK = true` the PTDE log says whether DXVK started and on which GPU, whether it may take exclusive fullscreen (and why not) and whether NVIDIA RTX HDR is filtering the picture, and DXVK's own `DARKSOULS_d3d9.log` is in the game folder. If the game freezes, the log says when the simulation stopped advancing; a crash writes a `*-crash.dmp` next to the DLL and a `CRASH:` line to the log. If a game script hits an error the game does not handle, the game closes itself; the log then has a `SCRIPT:` line saying which scripts failed and why. The heartbeat also shows how much memory the scripts use (`script memory MB (low-high)`). Send the log and any dump with a bug report.

The release DLLs leave out the development diagnostics (recordings, hardware watchpoints, the render thread sampler and their test hotkeys), so the INIs in their zips have no `[Diagnostics]` section: PTDE since v2.0.1, Remastered since v2.1.0. A diagnostics build has them (see Build), and so does Prepare to Co-op Edition.

## Build

Visual Studio 2022 (MSVC, `/MT`):

```
build.bat
```

builds both targets. `build_dsr.bat` and `build_ptde.bat` build one each. `build_ptde.bat` also downloads DXVK 3.1.1 from its GitHub release once (`tools\fetch_dxvk.ps1`, SHA-256 checked) and puts its 32-bit `d3d9.dll` in `build\ptde\dxvk_d3d9.dll`.

These are diagnostics builds: the `[Diagnostics]` INI keys work. `build.bat release` (or `build_dsr.bat release`, `build_ptde.bat release`) compiles the diagnostics out, as the published DLLs are (`FPSU_DIAGNOSTICS=0`, see `src\common\fpsu_buildcfg.h`). `package_release.bat` makes both release builds, checks that neither DLL imports the diagnostics' thread and keyboard functions or has a TLS section, and writes the two zips to `build\release` with the INIs minus `[Diagnostics]`. Both DLLs carry a version resource (`src\common\fpsu_version.h`).

## How it works

**Remastered.** The retail frame pacer counts frames as the performance counter times 60, measures how many have passed, and waits for the next one. The simulation, effect, Havok and menu steps are given a fixed 1/60 s each displayed frame. The mod gives the pacer's measurement and its wait one clock that runs at `MaxFPS`/60 of real time, so it paces at `MaxFPS`, and reports a frame that is only one to three frames late as on time instead of letting the game drop the next frame to catch up. It hands the steps the real time each frame took. The movement fixes scale the per-frame constants found in the executable.

**PTDE.** The game runs at a fixed 1/30 s step. The mod writes the measured frame time into that step every frame, caps the rate with a precise wait, and stops the render thread waiting two vertical blanks per frame (the change DSfix makes). `FPSLimitPoint` chooses the wait. `early` (the default) waits on the game thread at the start of each frame, before the controller, keyboard and mouse are read: the least input lag. `present` waits on the render thread immediately before the frame's first Present that shows a new picture (gameplay, loading screen or any other screen); the catch-up re-present is not waited on again. It gives the most even frame timing, with slightly more input lag. Only the named point waits. If the chosen point cannot be hooked (another mod, or an unexpected build), the cap waits where 1.8.0 did, when the render thread takes the next frame, so there is always a cap. The same physics constants were found in the PTDE executable by the same method, and are scaled with the frame time.

Both mods check the bytes they patch before writing, so on an unsupported build they refuse rather than corrupt anything.

**PTDE with DXVK.** The game and DSfix both bind to the Windows `d3d9.dll` before the mod loads, and DSfix hooks its `Direct3DCreate9`. When the game first calls it, the mod loads `dxvk_d3d9.dll`, has DXVK create its Direct3D 9 object once on a separate thread (DXVK ends the process if it cannot start, so this test runs where a failure only ends that thread), and only then makes the Windows `Direct3DCreate9` jump to DXVK just past its first instruction bytes. Whatever hooks that function, DSfix included, then wraps DXVK's objects instead of the Windows ones.

**PTDE: DXVK's frames through DXGI.** DXVK finds Vulkan through `GetProcAddress`; the mod points DXVK's import of it at its own, so each Vulkan function DXVK looks up passes through the mod. The window system calls (surface, swap chain, acquire, present) are answered by the mod: the swap chain images DXVK draws into are Direct3D 11 textures shared with Vulkan, and on each present Direct3D 11 waits on the GPU for DXVK's frame (a Direct3D 11 fence shared as a Vulkan timeline semaphore), copies it into a DXGI flip-model swap chain on the game window and presents it (tearing allowed in DXVK's immediate mode, for variable refresh). The submission that waits for an acquired image is changed to wait for Direct3D 11's copy of that image instead. Extensions this does not provide (present wait, swap chain maintenance, exclusive fullscreen, HDR metadata) are hidden from DXVK, so it uses the plain swap chain path.
